[Guildhall](https://guildhall.codestz.dev) turns a code repository into a living 3D world. Every folder becomes a village, the biggest package becomes the capital, the commit history grows the land season by season, and when AI agents work on the code, you watch them walk through town as heroes.

This series is about how I built it, and specifically about the parts where AI alone was not enough. I'll start with the decision that mattered most, because it is the one every developer using AI can apply tomorrow:

**Before you build the world, build the workshop. And give the AI eyes.**

## Version one shipped. Version two looked worse.

Guildhall V1 was live and people liked it: a warm, chunky, toy-like island with townsfolk, a camera that filmed "stories", and a little HUD.

<Figure
  src="/images/blog/guildhall/v1-diorama.webp"

  label="V1"
  caption="V1: small, warm, readable. It set the look everyone, including me, liked."
/>

So I planned a "serious" V2. A continuous continent instead of one island, smooth terrain, ring-shaped towns, density metrics, architecture decision records, milestone gates, and a team of AI agents working in parallel.

The engineering was real. Assets were imported at true scale, crowds ran in workers, performance budgets were met, tests were green.

Then one morning I put V2 next to V1, and the new world looked worse.

That attempt now lives on a branch called `archive/v2-attempt`, kept only as a reference for what not to do.

## Why the agents could not see the problem

Here is the uncomfortable part. The agents did exactly what the plan said. Milestones met, tests passing, metrics in range. When something looked off, they reworked it, and kept reworking it.

But nothing in that loop could say _"this is the wrong thing."_

A test can tell you a house is placed on a valid tile. It cannot tell you the town feels empty. A metric can tell you the forest covers 30% of the land. It cannot tell you the forest looks like green noise. Procedural worlds fail in **combinations**: a castle in a river, but only with rain at 2.5 and hills at 0. You will never write the test for that combination, because you never imagined it.

The AI was working blind. And honestly, so was I: I was reviewing plans and diffs instead of looking at the world.

## The turn: build the workshop first

At the restart I wrote one line in the plan: _the Studio is the key to getting this polished._

Instead of generating the world and hoping, we built the tools to see it, piece by piece:

### Pieces: everything at true scale

Every asset, from 18 KayKit and Kenney packs, browsable and searchable, standing next to a character for scale. Sounds boring. It solved the first real bug of V1: houses smaller than the people walking past them. Now one rule holds everywhere: the door is a character's height.

<Figure
  src="/images/blog/guildhall/studio-pieces.webp"
  alt="The Pieces browser: a grid of weapons and props with filters by pack on the left and a character silhouette for scale"
  label="Pieces"
/>

### Tile prefabs as data, and a composer to author them

This was the biggest unlock. Every hex tile (a house, a farm, a canteen with a beer garden, an archery range) is a JSON file: its pieces, where people sit, work and talk, and what they must walk around. A composer lets me move pieces with a gizmo and then **try** the tile, with characters standing on every spot.

### The hex map: knobs you can turn in seconds

A 2D map of the island with sliders for coast, hills, rivers, forest and repo size, which opens the same island in 3D with one click. This is where "castle in a river with rain 2.5 and hills 0" stops being a mystery and becomes a one-click repro.

<Figure
  src="/images/blog/guildhall/studio-hexmap.webp"
  alt="The hex map: a 2D island in hexagons with sliders for repo size, coast, hills, rivers and forest on the left"
  label="Hex map"
/>

### The grip matrix: a visual regression test

V1 had a nasty problem: characters held props wrong. Grips were hand-tuned per prop, and they broke as soon as an animation rolled the wrist. In V2, a grip is data (handle point, working edge, which hand) authored in a grip editor, and the **grip matrix** shows every held prop in every animation on one sheet.

<Figure
  src="/images/blog/guildhall/studio-grip-matrix.webp"

  label="Grip matrix"
  caption="One glance tells you if any prop, in any animation, is held wrong. A test can't do that. A sheet can."
/>

### Boards for decisions

Some decisions are taste, not engineering. So they got boards instead of debates: a scale board to choose proportions side by side, and a nature board to choose how dense and how colorful the land should be.

## Giving the AI eyes

The Studio was not only for me. This is the part I want every developer working with agents to steal.

Every tool in the Studio can be driven by a script. The page exposes a small debug API (`window.guildhall`) that lets a tool set the camera, jump the story clock, change the season, or wait until the world is ready. On top of it, a shot harness opens any URL in a headless browser and saves the frame.

So every agent task ended the same way:

<ProcessFlow
  title="How an agent task ends"
  steps={[
    {
      title: 'Build the change',
      description: 'Inside a contract: the files it owns, the behavior it must keep.',
    },
    {
      title: 'Shoot it',
      description:
        'Before and after, at the same viewport and pixel density, on phone and desktop.',
    },
    {
      title: 'Look at it',
      description: 'The agent opens its own screenshots and critiques them before reporting.',
    },
    {
      title: 'Prove it holds',
      description:
        'A test sweeps many seeds: no building over water, no one walking through walls.',
    },
    { title: 'Human review', description: 'I review contact sheets, not diffs.' },
  ]}
/>

That changes what the AI can do. Without eyes, an agent can only satisfy the checks you wrote. With screenshots it must look at, it can notice the things nobody wrote a check for. And the Studio gives it a safe place to experiment: try a tile in the composer, turn a knob on the hex map, compare three nature presets, all before anything touches the real world.

<Callout type="tip">
  If your AI is building something visual or generative, the most valuable code you will write is
  not the feature. It is the tool that lets the AI see the feature.
</Callout>

## What it bought us

The restart kept V2's good foundations (the asset pipeline, the renderer, the worker build, the crowd, the Studio) and threw away only the world generation and the look. A one-hour spike on a hex grid looked right immediately, and the Studio made it fast to build on.

## The lesson

Tests tell you the thing works. Only looking tells you it is the right thing.

AI did a huge share of the typing on Guildhall. But the turns that mattered came from someone looking at the real output and making a call, and the Studio is what made looking cheap, for me and for the agents. If you are building anything procedural, visual or AI-generated: **your editor and your visualizer are the quality engine of the product. Build them first.**
